Five years earlier, this work might have required a team of junior consultants working for days or weeks. The key economic change is not simply faster writing. It is a sharp fall in the cost of producing, coordinating, and testing knowledge.

This forecast argues that AI agents will fundamentally reshape Australia's consulting industry by 2030. The most important change will not be the elimination of consultants themselves, but the automation of many research, analysis, documentation, and coordination tasks that currently support consulting projects. Productivity is likely to rise significantly. Whether those gains become higher wages, lower client prices, or higher firm profits will depend on industry structure, organisational adaptation, and policy choices.

Central question

When AI makes advice cheaper to produce, who captures the gains?

Capabilities → economic channels

AI agents turn knowledge work into a production system.

Unlike passive tools that answer individual questions, AI agents can pursue goals across multiple steps: gathering information, using software, coordinating subtasks, and producing drafts for review. Stanford HAI (2025) documents rapid improvement in reasoning, coding, and knowledge-intensive tasks. In consulting, these capabilities reduce the cost of the activities that sit beneath strategic judgement.

Why consulting is exposed

Consulting is fundamentally an information-processing business. Its products are often recommendations, models, presentations, and decisions assembled from dispersed evidence. According to IBISWorld (2025), Australia's management consulting industry generated approximately A$45.9 billion in annual revenue and included around 94,910 businesses in 2025. The sector is both economically significant and unusually exposed to systems that can perform knowledge-intensive tasks.

PwC's Global AI Jobs Barometer (2025) reports that industries most exposed to AI recorded 27% growth in revenue per employee, compared with 9% in less exposed industries. This does not prove that AI alone caused the difference, but it supports a plausible mechanism: firms that reorganise around AI can produce more output with the same labour input.

Three channels of change

  1. Information costs fall. Research and routine analysis that once required teams of analysts may be completed in hours.
  2. Coordination costs fall. Scheduling, documentation, project tracking, and internal knowledge retrieval become easier to automate.
  3. Labour becomes more productive. A consultant supported by specialised agents can test more hypotheses and serve more clients.

The IMF (2024) estimates that AI could affect approximately 40% of jobs globally, with advanced economies facing the highest exposure because of their concentration of knowledge-intensive occupations. For consultants, exposure is likely to mean both greater capability and greater disruption.

Human labour scales the firm.

Large analyst cohorts conduct research and build the materials that support senior advice. Training occurs through repeated exposure to this work.

The training paradox

If firms automate junior work, they may reduce costs today while weakening the pathway that produces experienced managers and partners tomorrow.

AI may lower barriers to entry by giving small firms and independent consultants access to analytical capabilities that previously required large specialist teams. This could increase competition and make advice more affordable.

Yet AI may also strengthen incumbent firms. Large consultancies possess proprietary data, trusted brands, computing resources, and greater capacity to invest in secure systems. Digital technologies can lower entry barriers while increasing returns to scale, so the direction of concentration is not predetermined (Autor 2015).

Scenario analysis · 2030

Three futures are plausible. Policy shifts the probability of each.

These scenarios are not predictions with precise probabilities. They are structured ways to compare how capability progress, market concentration, organisational adaptation, and workforce policy interact.

Baseline scenario

AI as a co-worker

AI agents become standard tools across major consulting firms. Routine research, benchmarking, and documentation are increasingly automated, while people focus on client relationships and strategic judgement. Productivity rises and firms continue hiring graduates, though in smaller numbers.

Policy playbook

Technology sets possibilities. Institutions shape outcomes.

The future of consulting will not be determined by capability progress alone. Governments, universities, regulators, and firms can influence who is prepared, who can compete, and who bears the transition costs.

01

Universities · firms · 2026–2028

Redesign business education

Integrate AI-agent supervision, workflow design, verification, and human–AI collaboration into business and consulting programs. Preserve project-based learning and client-facing experience so automation does not remove the practice through which judgement develops.

Methods, data, and uncertainty

This is a mechanism-based forecast, not a precise prediction.

Method

This forecast focuses on 2025–2030 and applies a capability → economic channel → outcome framework. It begins with observable improvements in AI systems, identifies the consulting tasks those systems could affect, and then traces the implications for productivity, employment, industry structure, and distribution. Evidence is drawn from academic research, government and international organisations, industry reports, and AI capability reporting.

The scenarios vary four key assumptions: the speed of AI capability progress, the cost and accessibility of deployment, the degree of organisational adaptation, and the strength of policy responses. The interactive gains model is intentionally illustrative. It does not estimate actual shares of future income; it demonstrates why competition, bargaining power, and skill access matter.

Uncertainty and limitations

Forecasting AI is inherently uncertain because technological capabilities, organisational adoption, worker responses, regulation, and macroeconomic conditions evolve together. Frank et al. (2019) argue that these interactions make labour-market effects especially difficult to predict. Current productivity evidence may not generalise to all consulting firms, and reported exposure does not necessarily become automation. Proprietary data, client trust, legal responsibility, and the reliability of agents may slow adoption.

There is also limited evidence about how organisations will redesign career pathways when junior tasks are automated. A technical breakthrough could accelerate change, while regulation, security failures, or weak client demand could slow it. The scenarios should therefore be read as plausible futures that help decision-makers prepare, not as point forecasts.
